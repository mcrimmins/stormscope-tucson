About this project

Testing AI storm nowcasts for the next 0–3 hours in southern Arizona

StormScope Tucson runs an artificial-intelligence weather model, trained only on satellite, radar and lightning observations, to show where thunderstorms are likely to form and move over the next three hours. It is a research prototype from the University of Arizona Cooperative Extension climate program, built to find out whether this new kind of model is useful for monsoon forecasting in our desert and mountain terrain.

Why the next few hours matter

Monsoon hazards develop fastFlash floods in washes and burn scars, lightning, damaging winds and dust can go from clear skies to dangerous in under an hour.
Radar extrapolation fades quicklyMoving today's radar echoes forward works for an hour or so, but misses storms that have not formed yet, which is most monsoon storms.
Weather models are costlyPhysics-based storm-scale models are powerful but need large computers. An AI model like this one runs on a single desktop graphics card.

How it works

1 · ObserveLatest observationsGOES-19 satellite imagery (8 infrared and water-vapor channels), MRMS radar mosaic and GLM lightning, every 10 minutes.
2 · ForecastNVIDIA StormScopeA generative AI model predicts the next satellite and radar images in 10-minute steps on a 3 km grid, out to 3 hours.
3 · Ensemble6 possible outcomesEach run makes 6 different, equally plausible forecasts. Where they agree, confidence is higher.
4 · ProbabilitiesMaps you can useWe turn the 16 forecasts into chances of storms, heavy cores and lightning within about 10 km, by hour and by location.

Runs cover a 670 × 650 km box from the Mogollon Rim into northern Sonora, centered on the NWS Tucson forecast area. Each 6-member, 3-hour run takes about 11 minutes on one NVIDIA RTX 3090 at the University of Arizona.

What the research shows

StormScope was introduced by NVIDIA researchers with NOAA and university collaborators in 2026 and trained on observations over the continental U.S. from 2018–2023. In their evaluation of severe-weather days in March–August 2025:

~75%higher critical success index than NOAA's Warn-on-Forecast System for echoes ≥40 dBZ at 30 minutes (about 45% at 2 hours)
900+forecasts (929) compared with the Warn-on-Forecast System, the NowcastNet AI model and radar extrapolation
New stormsanticipated after the forecast starts, not just existing echoes moved forward

Source: Pathak et al. (2026), arXiv:2601.17268 (v2, Nov 2026), a preprint not yet peer reviewed. Technical details and full references are in the technical deep dive below.

What we are testing here

Published results are averaged over the whole country. Southern Arizona is different, so we are checking how well the model does here before anyone relies on it:

Sky Island stormsDo forecasts start storms over the Catalinas, Rincons and Huachucas at the right time, and move them into the valleys correctly?
Storms from MexicoU.S. radar coverage ends near the border, so the model sees storms in Sonora mainly through satellite imagery.
Storm strengthEarly tests place storms well but weaken the strongest cores after the first hour. We show heavy-core chances for the first hour only until this is better understood.
Past eventsWe rerun notable 2026 monsoon and fall storm days and compare each forecast with what radar observed. See Case studies in the viewer.

Skill is measured with the fractions skill score (how well forecast and observed storm areas overlap within a neighborhood) and compared with persistence, a forecast that simply holds the current radar still.

Our results so far

We reran 12 events (July 2024, the 2026 monsoon and the Halloween 2026 storm) and compared every 10-minute forecast step with observed MRMS radar. Scores are fractions skill scores within 33 km; persistence holds the starting radar still.

StormScope beat persistence for rain and storm areas in 12 of 12 events at 1 hour, 11 of 12 at 2 hours and 8 of 12 at 3 hours. Forecast storms gradually weaken: by 3 hours the forecast covers about half the observed storm area, and the strongest cores fade after the first hour. That is why this site stops at 3 hours and limits the strong-core product to the first hour.

Setup matters: running on a tight box around Arizona made storms die out within 2 hours. Giving the model about 750 km of surrounding context (to the Gulf of California, Sonora and the Four Corners) fixed most of that, matching a full-CONUS run at a fraction of the cost. Runs use 6 ensemble members of the StormScope Flash model.

Limitations and appropriate use

This is an experimental tool. Use it alongside, never instead of, National Weather Service forecasts and warnings. It forecasts satellite, radar and lightning signals only, not wind, blowing dust, outflow boundaries or rainfall totals. Satellite views of Arizona come from GOES-East at an angle, which can shift cloud tops slightly west of their true location. Products may be late or missing if observations or computing are interrupted. Check the data age shown in the viewer.
Technical deep dive

How StormScope works, and how we run it

This section is for partners who want the details behind the forecasts. Numbers about the model and the published evaluation come from Pathak et al. (2026) [1] and NVIDIA's model card [2]; numbers about southern Arizona come from our own tests.

1. A forecast learned from observations, not physics equations

Conventional storm-scale models such as the HRRR or the Warn-on-Forecast System (WoFS) [9] solve the equations of atmospheric physics from an analyzed starting state. StormScope instead learns how satellite, radar and lightning observations evolve from one 10-minute image to the next, using six years of past observations. No numerical model is used for training or to start a forecast, so it can start the moment new observations arrive.

2. Inputs

  • Satellite: eight GOES-R ABI channels [6]: visible 0.47, 0.64 and 0.86 µm; shortwave window 3.9 µm; water vapor 6.19, 6.95 and 7.34 µm; clean infrared window 10.35 µm.
  • Radar: MRMS maximum composite and base reflectivity [5].
  • Lightning: GOES Geostationary Lightning Mapper (GLM) [7] event counts in 10-minute windows.
  • Static and time information: terrain height, a radar-coverage map, latitude/longitude and the solar angle.
  • History: the last hour of observations, six frames 10 minutes apart. Everything is placed on the 3 km HRRR grid.

3. Model architecture

StormScope is a pair of diffusion transformers [3, 4] of about 260 million parameters each: one for satellite imagery, one for radar and lightning. A diffusion model generates an image by starting from random noise and removing it step by step, guided by the recent observations, so each run draws a different but physically plausible future. To make attention affordable on a 1024 × 1792 grid, the model uses neighborhood attention [8], where each patch only attends to nearby patches. Separate "expert" networks handle the coarse, large-scale structure and the fine convective detail.

4. How a forecast is made

  • At each 10-minute step the satellite model predicts the next set of GOES images from the last hour.
  • The radar/lightning model then predicts reflectivity and lightning, conditioned on the satellite forecast.
  • Both predictions are appended to the one-hour window and the oldest frame is dropped (an autoregressive rollout), repeating out to the lead time needed.
  • Running the whole chain several times with different random noise gives an ensemble of possible outcomes.

5. What the published evaluation found [1]

  • 929 forecasts on about 60 severe-weather days, March–August 2025, compared with WoFS (bias-corrected), the NowcastNet AI model [10] and radar extrapolation including pySTEPS [11].
  • Critical success index for echoes ≥40 dBZ was about 76% higher than WoFS at 30 minutes and 45% higher at 2 hours. Probability of detecting ≥40 dBZ echoes was 75/64/50% at 30/60/120 minutes, versus 47/40/34% for WoFS.
  • It detected storms that formed shortly after the forecast started better than WoFS, which the authors attribute partly to growing cumulus seen by satellite.
  • Forecasters rated the forecasts a median 8 out of 10 in 111 subjective evaluations.
  • Stated limits: skill converges toward WoFS by about 3 hours, ensembles become too confident (underdispersive) at longer leads, it does not predict hail or wind, and longer leads likely need larger-scale weather context.
  • The evaluation days were chosen for severe-weather potential, mostly outside the Southwest; monsoon storms over desert terrain were not specifically tested.

6. How we run it for southern Arizona

  • Model: StormScope Flash (3 km, 10 minutes) via NVIDIA Earth2Studio 0.20 [12], under the NVIDIA Open Model License [13]. Flash is a distilled version that needs 5–7 model evaluations per step instead of about 100, which is what makes it practical on one desktop GPU.
  • Domain: a 670 × 650 km forecast area (29.5–35.5°N, 114.5–107.5°W) with about 750 km of surrounding context (padding of 250 grid points) so storms and moisture can move in from the Gulf of California, Sonora and the Four Corners.
  • Ensemble: 6 members, 18 steps (3 hours), about 11 minutes per run on one NVIDIA RTX 3090 (24 GB), using bfloat16 precision.
  • Data: GOES-East (GOES-19 since April 2025), MRMS and GLM from the NOAA Open Data Dissemination program on Amazon Web Services.
  • Products: the chance that at least one member has reflectivity above 20, 35 or 45 dBZ, or lightning, within about 10 km of each point (a neighborhood probability), recalculated every 10 minutes of forecast time.

7. How we verify it

We reran 12 past events and compared each 10-minute forecast with observed MRMS reflectivity. The main score is the fractions skill score (FSS) [14]: it compares the fraction of an area covered by storms in the forecast and in the observations within a neighborhood (33 km here), so a storm forecast slightly out of place still gets partial credit; 1 is perfect and 0 has no skill. The baseline is persistence, the starting radar image held still. Frequency bias is forecast storm area divided by observed storm area: 1 is ideal, below 1 means storms are under-forecast.

8. What we learned while setting it up

  • Context matters most. With only about 75 km of surrounding context, forecast storms faded to about 16% of the observed storm area by 2 hours. With about 750 km of context, the same forecasts kept about 70%, matching a full-CONUS run.
  • Flash trades strength for speed. On a test day, the full (non-distilled) model kept storm area close to observed through 3 hours and scored about four times better than persistence for thunderstorms at 3 hours, where Flash steadily weakened storms. But the full model only runs over all of CONUS and takes about 1 h 45 min for a 3-hour forecast on our GPU, too slow for live use. A comparison over all 12 events is underway.
  • Where it is strongest: placing and moving rain and storm areas over the first 2 hours. Where it is weakest: isolated new storms over the Sky Islands west of the main activity, strong cores after the first hour, and evening decay.

Glossary

  • dBZ: radar reflectivity. Roughly, 20 dBZ is light rain, 35 dBZ a thunderstorm, 45 dBZ and up heavy rain and possibly hail.
  • Nowcast: a forecast for the next 0–6 hours built mainly from current observations.
  • Ensemble: several equally plausible forecasts; their agreement indicates confidence.
  • Neighborhood probability: the share of ensemble members with an event anywhere within a set distance of a point.
  • Critical success index (CSI): hits divided by hits plus misses plus false alarms.
